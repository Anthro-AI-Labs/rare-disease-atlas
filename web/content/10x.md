# The milestone

Launch a shared natural history study across 2-3 gene communities that share biology (for example STXBP1 and SYNGAP1), by reusing registries and study designs that already exist instead of starting from zero.

# Sharing already happens

These records were checked on ClinicalTrials.gov on 2026-10-04:

- STARR (NCT06555965): one natural history study covers both STXBP1 and SYNGAP1. It is run by Children's Hospital of Philadelphia with the STXBP1 Foundation at 5 sites, with 600 participants planned. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06555965
- EMBOLD (NCT05818553): one drug, PRAX-562, is tested in SCN2A and SCN8A encephalopathy in the same trial, at 8 sites. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05818553
- FENDEEP (NCT05232630): one fenfluramine pilot trial enrolled people with several conditions, including STXBP1 and SYNGAP1 (20 participants, completed 2024). https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05232630
- Simons Searchlight (NCT01238250): one registry lists over 150 genetic conditions, including STXBP1, SCN2A and SYNGAP1. https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01238250

Shared studies are already normal practice. The hard part is finding them and finding the right partner gene.

# Today's route

- Step 1. Find related communities and researchers. We found no published measure of how long this takes (see our own measurement below).
- Step 2. Find existing registries and study designs to reuse. No published measure either (see our own measurement below).
- Step 3. Judge whether they are comparable. This needs an expert. No published measure; team estimate only.
- Step 4. Write the protocol, get ethics approval and start up sites. Median 255 days from protocol delivery to first participant enrolled, and median 132 days for regulatory approval alone (North American cardiovascular trials). Source: JAMA Network Open 2021;4(7):e2117963, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9435961/
- Step 4, a rare epilepsy example. The ESCO STXBP1 study was registered on 2024-09-04 and enrolled its first participant on 2025-11-04, about 14 months later. Source: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06625112
- Step 5. Recruit families. Low enrollment was the most common reason for stopping rare disease trials, at 26% of terminations in 2016-2020. Source: Global Genes, reporting a GlobalData analysis (secondary source), https://globalgenes.org/?p=361022

# Our route with the Atlas

What the Atlas shortens:

- Step 1. Each disease page shows related conditions and the patient groups for each gene. For example, the STXBP1 page lists the STXBP1 Foundation and ESCO, plus groups for related conditions.
- Step 2. Each page lists registries, natural history studies and trials with a source link. The Atlas currently holds 15 curated patient groups and 69 curated assets for 8 genes.
- Step 3, only in part. The Atlas shows the evidence behind every link, labels computed links as "hypothesis only" and lists "needs expert review" items. It does not replace the expert.

What the Atlas does not shorten:

- Step 4. Ethics approval, contracts and site start-up take the same time as before.
- Step 5. Recruitment depends on families and clinics. Software does not remove this bottleneck.

# Where the speed-up comes from

Our own measured number: one team member built the patient group list (15 organizations) and the assets list (69 studies, registries and models) for 8 genes in about 2 hours of focused work. This was with AI search help, followed by checking every link by hand. On the way we found and fixed real errors: wrong addresses, a closed organization, broken sign-up links and a suspended registry. The Atlas now shows the same information for a gene in seconds.

- For steps 1-2, the speed-up is roughly hours to seconds, well over 10x. This counts only one person who already knew where to look.
- The catch: it is fast only because someone curated the data once. That work is done once and then reused by every family and researcher who opens the page.

For the whole milestone the gain is much smaller. Site start-up alone takes a median of 255 days, about 36 weeks. If discovery and comparison take D weeks today and close to zero with the Atlas:

- Overall speed-up = (D + 36 + recruitment time) / (36 + recruitment time)
- Even if D = 12 weeks (team estimate) and recruitment took no time at all, that is 48 / 36, about 1.3x.

Honest summary: the Atlas gives a large speed-up for finding and connecting (well over 10x on our own measurement), but probably under 2x for launching a shared study, because ethics approval, start-up and recruitment dominate. The bigger win may come from joining an existing study such as STARR instead of building a new one. We have no source for how much time that saves, so it is listed as an assumption.

# Assumptions

- Families in different gene communities are willing to share data and join one study.
- Registries collect data in compatible ways (similar outcome measures and formats). This is not checked.
- A shared mechanism makes a shared study design reasonable. This needs expert review. The Atlas shows computed links as "hypothesis only" for this reason.
- "Discovery takes D weeks today" is a team estimate. We found no published measure of how long families or researchers spend finding related communities and studies.
- Adding a gene cohort to an existing study is faster than starting a new one. This is a team estimate with no source.
- Our 2-hour measurement comes from one team member with AI help, for 8 genes. It may not hold for other genes or for a parent without research experience.

# What must be validated next

- An expert confirms one link. Do STXBP1 and SYNGAP1 share enough biology for one study design? STARR suggests yes; we should ask the STARR team directly.
- One patient group agrees to a pilot conversation. For example, ask the STXBP1 Foundation or CURE SYNGAP1 whether the Atlas showed them a study or partner they did not already know.
- Time a real user. Ask one parent to find the same information with and without the Atlas and record both times. This replaces our team estimate with a measured number.
